Bands and frequencies

A band is a slice of the radio spectrum set aside for amateurs. The rules about what can be done on each band, and by which class of licence, are different in each country. Here are the bands I use in Millbrook, and what you can hear there. The frequencies are in megahertz (MHz). Always check the current rules before transmitting: this table is a note for a friend, not a legal document.

The Millbrook repeater

The club repeater is on the hill above the water tower. Output 146.760 MHz, input 146.160 MHz (minus 600 kHz), access tone 100 Hz. It covers the whole lake and half the valley. Please leave a gap of two seconds between talking so that others can break in. If you hear a call for help, say "break" and listen.

Propagation notes

Why are the bands different at different times? Radio waves on the high frequencies bounce off a layer of the upper atmosphere, which is ionised by the sun. During the day the layer is thicker and the higher bands (15, 10 metres) work; at night the layer thins and the lower bands (80, 40) carry far. The 11-year sunspot cycle changes it all: 1999 is a good year, and 10 metres opens more every month. Keep an ear on 28.200 MHz at noon.

The beacons. A beacon is a small transmitter that sends its call sign over and over, so that you can tell if the band is open. I listen to the beacons on 28.200 MHz with a notebook. When I can hear four or five, I get on the air.

Q-codes I use every day: QRM (interference by other stations), QRN (interference by nature), QSB (the signal goes up and down), QRP (low power), QTH (my location), QSL (message received; also a card), QSY (change frequency), QRT (stop transmitting). "73" means best wishes. "88" means love and kisses; I use it only with my wife.
