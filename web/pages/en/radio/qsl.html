QSL cards

A QSL card is a postcard that two radio amateurs exchange to confirm a contact. It carries the callsign, the date and time, the frequency, the report and, on the picture side, something from the sender's town. They are small pieces of paper from far away, and I have 600 of them. Here are some from my wall.

My own card has a drawing of the Millbrook iron bridge in black on yellow, made by my niece when she was nine. On the back there are the lines to fill in, and the words "Thanks for the QSO, 73".

How to get a card

The usual way is to send your own card first, with a self-addressed envelope and a small contribution for the postage (in the international post, a card from abroad needs a coupon, or the cost of a stamp). The other way is the QSL bureau: the national radio society collects the cards and sends them in parcels to the other societies. It is slow, a year or two, but it is cheap and it works.

The wall. I keep the cards in a binder with plastic pockets, sorted by prefix. The best ones are pinned on the wall. The one with the pigeon is next to the window, where it can see the lake.

What is the best card I have received? A hand-drawn card from a man in a small town on the coast, drawn in ballpoint pen, of a boat. At the bottom: "Thank you for waiting for me to find the right words in English." We had a conversation of forty minutes. I will keep that one.
