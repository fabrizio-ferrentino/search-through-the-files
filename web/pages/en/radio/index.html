Welcome to my shack, on the air from Millbrook since 1983. My name is Harvey, I am forty-eight, and I work in the day for the water board. In the evening I come down to the spare room under the roof, switch on the radio and listen to the world. This page is my logbook, my notebook and my way of saying thank you to the people who answered when I called.

What is amateur radio? It is a hobby, and a service: anyone who passes the examination can transmit on bands set aside by the government for the purpose, talk to other amateurs in the next street or on the other side of the world, build their own equipment, and help when the telephones are down. We do not make money from it, and we are not allowed to. We make friends, and now and then a very good antenna.

Bands and frequencies

A band is a slice of the radio spectrum set aside for amateurs. Here are some of the bands I use in Millbrook. Always check the current rules before transmitting: this table is a note for a friend, not a legal document.

Band MHz What I hear there
80 m 3.500 - 3.800 Evening chats between neighbours, a net on Sundays at 9:00 AM
40 m 7.000 - 7.200 My favourite for local friends. Busy after dark; contests at weekends
20 m 14.000 - 14.350 The long-distance band. Opens to the east in the afternoon, to the west in the evening
10 m 28.000 - 29.700 A zoo when open. When closed, nothing. Beacons in the first part
2 m 144.000 - 148.000 The local band: repeaters, the club net, packet mailbox

The Millbrook repeater is on the hill above the water tower. Output 146.760 MHz, input 146.160 MHz, access tone 100 Hz. It covers the whole lake and half the valley. Leave a gap of two seconds between talking so that others can break in. If you hear a call for help, say "break" and listen. Q-codes I use every day: QRM (interference by other stations), QRN (interference by nature), QSB (the signal goes up and down), QRP (low power), QTH (my location), QSL (message received; also a card). "73" means best wishes. "88" means love and kisses; I use it only with my wife.

Build a 20 metre dipole

The dipole is the most useful antenna that you can build at home: two pieces of wire, a centre insulator, two end insulators and a coaxial cable. Mine has worked every evening for four years, through two storms and one very angry magpie. The formula: total length in feet = 468 divided by the frequency in MHz. For 14.2 MHz that is 33 feet, so each side is 16 feet 6 inches. Cut a little longer and tune by shortening.

  • Cut two pieces of wire, each 17 feet. Connect the centre of the coaxial cable to one wire and the screen to the other, at the middle insulator. Wrap the joints well: rain gets in everywhere.
  • Tie the end of each wire to an end insulator, fix a rope to each and raise the antenna as high as you can.
  • Measure the SWR at 14.1, 14.2 and 14.3 MHz. If it is lowest at the bottom of the band, the antenna is too long; if lowest at the top, too short. Cut about one inch at a time from each end. A good antenna has a ratio below 1.5.

Safety. Never put up an antenna where it may fall on a power line. Never. Not even once. Disconnect the coaxial cable from the radio during thunderstorms. My wife holds the ladder and makes a comment about every step. My 10 metre vertical is an aluminium tube on a wooden post. It works better than it looks. It looks like a curtain rod.

CB corner

Citizens' Band radio is the little brother of amateur radio. No examination is needed. It works on 27 MHz with a few watts and a short antenna, and it is used by truckers, farmers, builders and everybody who wants to talk to a neighbour without paying for a telephone. I started on CB at fifteen, with a set from a flea market. Around here: channel 9 (27.065 MHz) is for EMERGENCIES ONLY and the club monitors it in the evenings; the farmers of the valley are on 27.125, the trucks on the main road on 27.185, the boat people of the lake on 27.265. A normal call goes: "Breaker one-nine, this is Lakeside Harvey, anyone around the bridge?" and then you listen. The answer may come at once or never. It is the charm of the thing. I have never heard anyone say "good buddy" except as a joke.
