The checklist: twenty things, in order

Print this page and put it on the refrigerator. Tick each box with a pencil. The things are in order of importance, as I see it, not in order of difficulty. Start with the first one and do not skip.

[ ] What to do When
[ ] Write a list of everything in the house that has a clock, a date or a chip in it by April
[ ] Find the instruction books of all of it. Put them in one drawer by April
[ ] Call or write to the makers of anything expensive. Keep the answers by May
[ ] Test the computer. First save your files on floppy disks by June
[ ] Test the video recorder and the answering machine by June
[ ] Ask the bank, in writing, how it prepares for the year 2000 by July
[ ] Keep a paper copy of all bank and insurance statements from now on from now
[ ] Ask your doctor whether the machines at the clinic are tested by July
[ ] Check your car if it has a computer. Most older cars have none by August
[ ] Check the heating: ask the maker of the boiler about the control panel by August
[ ] Make a first-aid kit and a box of medicines for a week by September
[ ] Fill a box with candles, matches, batteries and a radio with batteries by October
[ ] Buy a stove that works without electricity, and gas for it by October
[ ] Store water: ten litres per person, in closed bottles by November
[ ] Make a list of telephone numbers on paper, not only in the machine by November
[ ] Take out a small amount of cash, enough for a week December
[ ] Fill the car with petrol in the middle of December, not on the 31st December
[ ] Talk to the neighbours: who has a generator, who has a doctor, who has a gas stove December
[ ] Switch off and unplug the computer and the video before midnight December 31
[ ] Celebrate the new year. Nothing may happen. That would be the best result December 31

Why that order? The first six are about knowing what you have. You cannot be afraid of the right thing until you know what you own. The next ones are about money and papers, because a paper copy needs no clock. The rest is about the week after the new year: if the power goes off, the water stops and the shops close, how do you manage? The answer is, as you did your grandmother's winters: with candles, a stove and neighbours.

What I do not put in the list: I do not tell you to leave town, to buy a farm, to withdraw all your savings or to bury gold in the garden. I have read pages that say these things. I have not understood them. If the bank cannot work, a farm will not help you much either.

Appliances: what I tested in my own house

I went through the house with a pencil and wrote down everything with a clock. It was thirty-one things. Here are some of the ones I have tested or written to the makers about. The brands are the ones in my house; yours will be different. A verdict of OK only means that it worked for me, on the day I tested it. I am not a laboratory.

Wexley VX 520 video recorder Yes, two digits FAILED Set to 12/31/1999, went past midnight and showed the old date 01/01/1900. The recording timer stops working. Maker says: buy a new one.
Dunmore 900 microwave oven Only a timer OK Counts minutes, not days. I tested it with a cup of tea.
Kellmar Z1 heating controller Yes, calendar WAIT The maker says it will work, the installer is not sure. Test planned for December 31.
Orchard Gate garage door None OK Passed on 03/24/1999. It has a chain, a motor and no opinions.
Home PC, Hartley 586 Yes, BIOS clock FAILED Set to December 31, 1999, 11:58 PM. After midnight the BIOS shows 1980. Files are safe; the spreadsheet is not.

What does "compliant" mean? It means that the maker says the product will work correctly with dates after December 31, 1999. It does not mean that the maker has tested it. I asked a maker for a written statement and received a card with a smiling sun on it and the words "Our products are ready for the future." I keep it as a souvenir.

Things I did not test: the lift in the town hall (the janitor says it is safe), the traffic lights at the bridge (the council says it is safe), my pacemaker (I do not have one, but my uncle does, and he says the cardiologist laughed).

Money and papers

My bank is a small one, on the square. The manager is a nice man who told me with a smile that "everything has been taken care of". I asked if he could write that down. He said he would think about it. That is where my worries about money began.

  • Ask your bank, in writing: is your system ready for the year 2000, on what date was it tested, who is responsible if there is an error in my account, what will happen to my cash card on January 1? Keep the reply with the statements.
  • Keep a paper record. Ask for a printed statement every month and keep them in a box. In January 2000 compare the last statement of 1999 with the first of 2000.
  • Cash. A small amount at home, enough for a week of shopping, is wise at any time of the year. I do not tell you to withdraw everything: that would be silly and would also make the bank worry.
  • Pension, taxes, insurance. The pension office says its computers are ready; I have written to ask in which way. Keep a copy of every tax form. Some old insurance policies have a date of expiry in two digits, and a computer may think the policy ended in 1900.
  • The cash machine outside my bank has a sticker that says "Year 2000 ready". My plan is to withdraw a small sum on January 2, and one on January 3, and see if the number of the receipt makes sense.
