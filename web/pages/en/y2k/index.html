On January 1, 2000, at midnight, many computers will think it is the year 1900. This is not a joke, and it is not science fiction. It is a plain fact, known to engineers since the 1970s, which the newspapers discovered about two years ago: for decades programmers wrote the year with two digits to save memory, when memory was expensive. "99" means 1999. But what comes after "99"? For a computer with two digits, it is "00". And "00" can be read as 1900.

Nobody knows exactly what will happen. Some experts say "nothing", some say "a few annoying problems", and a few say "the end of the world". I am an ordinary person and I do not know. What I know is that I would rather be ready for nothing than be unready for something. That is why I wrote this page.

DO NOT PANIC. BUT DO NOT SLEEP EITHER.

The five things everybody should do now
  1. Ask the makers of your computer, video recorder and car whether they are "Year 2000 compliant". Write down the answer, with the name of the person who gave it.
  2. Test your computer: set the date to December 31, 1999, 11:58 PM, wait for midnight, and see what the machine says. Before doing it, save everything on a floppy disk.
  3. Take a printout of every bank statement and every insurance paper this year. A copy on paper is a copy that does not need a clock.
  4. Buy some candles, a gas stove for camping, and water. A week is enough. Do not buy a generator unless you really need one.
  5. Talk to your neighbours. The best protection is having a neighbour who knows your name.

Supplies for a week, without exaggerating

I have been through two winters with the power off for three days (the 1986 snow and the 1991 flood). That is my only qualification. Here is what I keep in a box in the cellar for a family of four.

Item How much Cost
Drinking water in closed bottles 80 litres $24
Tinned food (beans, tomatoes, fish, fruit) about 40 tins $45
Pasta, rice, flour, biscuits 6 kg $12
Camping stove and gas cartridges 1 stove, 6 cartridges $38
Candles (the long-burning kind) 20 $10
Torches and spare batteries 2 torches, 12 batteries $16
Radio with batteries 1 $14
First-aid kit and usual medicines 1 box $20

Total: about $180. The money is not wasted: everything will be used in a power cut, a flood, or a camping holiday. Water: about two litres of drinking water a day per person. The cold tap will probably keep working, because the water supply of Millbrook is gravity-fed from the hill reservoir, as the town hall's engineer told me, but one cannot be sure of the pumps. Heat: do not use a barbecue or a gas ring indoors to warm a room; every winter some people die of it. Use blankets. Food that needs no cooking is good to keep even if nothing happens: bread, jam, tinned fish, dried fruit, chocolate. Chocolate is for morale. Pets: a week's food for the cat or the dog. Mine is a cat called Pixel; he is not worried.

Letters from readers

Since January I have received thirty-one letters about this page, one of them in green ink. Here are some, with my answers.

Margaret, Millbrook, 03/27/1999: My son says it is all a hoax invented by computer firms to sell new computers. Is he right?
Roy: Partly. Some firms do sell new things with fear. But the problem is real; it is just smaller than they say, and more spread out than the newspapers say. Do the checklist and sleep well.

Giovanni, Lakeview, 03/22/1999: I have a very old computer, from 1986. Will it die at midnight?
Roy: It probably has only two digits for the year, so it will show a strange date. But it will not explode. Save your files, check the date at the new year, and set it by hand.

Anna, 03/08/1999: I am afraid of the lift in my building, which is very old. Should I be?
Roy: Lifts have mechanical locks. If the electronics stop, they stop; they do not fall. Ask the administrator if the lift has been tested. If it does not satisfy you, take the stairs, which is healthy anyway.

About me and this page

My name is Roy Dalton. I am fifty-four, I live in Millbrook, and until 1997 I was a heating engineer. I am not a computer expert. I own one computer, bought in 1996, and I learned to make a web page from a book from the library that had three pages torn out.

In January 1998 I saw a programme on television about the year 2000 problem. The man on the screen said that "nobody knows". I went to the cellar, found the control panel of my boiler, and read the booklet. It said nothing. That was when I got worried. I put it on the Web because my wife told me that if I talked about it once more at dinner she would leave. She has not left.

What I do not know: whether the power stations are ready (the company says yes; I would like to see the cellar), whether the traffic lights are ready, and what the newspapers will say on January 2. Thank you to the librarian, who found me the book, to my neighbour who let me test his cash register, and to my cat, who has no opinions about the year 2000 and no wish to have any.
